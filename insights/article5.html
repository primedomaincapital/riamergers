The Streak Is Over: RIA M&A Fell 17%, and the Mega-Sellers Didn’t Notice

Research · Prime Domain Capital

On October 15, 2026, DeVoe & Company released its third-quarter RIA Deal Book, and the first number in it stopped a seven-quarter run. The industry announced 77 transactions in the third quarter, down 17% from 93 a year earlier, ending a streak in which every quarter for nearly two years had set or tied a record for its period. The report called it the clearest break in the market’s upward trajectory since activity plateaued in 2022 and 2023. The second number in the report told a different story entirely: through September, the industry had announced 244 transactions, slightly ahead of last year’s record pace. The quarter fell. The year did not.

The third number explained the contradiction. Sellers with more than $5 billion in assets under management, the firms DeVoe calls mega sellers, completed 41 transactions through September. That already exceeds every full year on record, and it represents a record 17% share of the market. The market did not retreat in the third quarter. It bifurcated: the largest firms kept selling, and the smallest stopped.

Who is buying, and who stopped selling

Consolidators announced 129 deals through September, a 53% share of the market, up from 45% in 2024 and 49% in 2025. RIA buyers took 29% of deals. The remaining category, private equity, broker-dealers, and banks, dropped to 18%, the lowest since 2014. The buyer universe is narrowing to the firms whose business is buying firms.

The seller side narrowed in the opposite direction. Small sellers, firms with $100 million to $500 million in assets, held a 36% share through September, putting them on pace for their lowest full-year share on record. Excluding mega deals, the average seller managed $1.02 billion and the median seller managed $625 million. The middle of the market, the $1 billion firm that was the classic acquisition target for a decade, is now the median transaction, and the small firm that once supplied the volume has gone quiet.

DeVoe’s founder and chief executive, David DeVoe, said the slowdown surprised the firm and sent it back to the drawing board. The explanation it settled on was volatility: spikes in the CBOE Volatility Index, the U.S.-Iran conflict in March 2026, tariffs, and overheated AI valuations led owners to postpone sales. His warning about reading the numbers is worth repeating: every announced deal reflects a decision to sell made six to 18 months earlier. The third quarter’s 77 deals are a photograph of decisions made in late 2025 and early 2026, not a survey of what owners think today.

The other trackers tell a similar story with different counts. Piper Sandler counted 89 transactions in the third quarter, down from 93 a year earlier, in data the bank compiled for Wealth Management. The counts differ because the populations differ: DeVoe tracks sales of independent RIAs with at least $100 million in assets. Neither count reports third-quarter valuations, and neither should be read as a price index. Transaction volume describes how many deals were announced. It does not establish a market-wide price for an individual firm.

The family office footnote that matters

One more number from the quarter deserves attention. FINTRX tracked 342 family office transactions worth $68.3 billion in disclosed round capital in the third quarter of 2026, and 49 of them, 14%, were RIA acquisitions. The figure is total round size, not what family offices themselves committed, so it should not be quoted as a pile of family money chasing advisors. But the direction is real: family offices are buying wealth managers, and they are doing it while the traditional private equity buyer steps back.

A DeVoe survey covered in August found only 22% of RIA leaders say their successors could afford a buyout. That number is the succession crisis in one line. The firms that cannot sell internally must sell externally, and the external buyers are increasingly consolidators who can pay with equity, absorb the operations, and offer the selling advisors a continuing role. The internal succession path is closing. The consolidator path is widening.

The counterargument: a pause, not a reversal

The case for calm is strong, and the numbers support it. Year to date, the industry is still slightly ahead of last year’s record pace. DeVoe now expects 320 to 340 deals for the full year, against 2025’s record of 322. It needs 79 fourth-quarter deals to make 2026 a record on its count, fewer than the 81 it counted in each of the fourth quarters of 2025 and 2024. A 17% quarterly decline that leaves the annual forecast above the record is a pause, not a reversal, and pauses have happened before: the market plateaued in 2022 and 2023 and then set seven straight quarterly records.

There is also a fair criticism of the streak narrative itself. Seven quarters of records is a low bar when the market grows every year: each quarter is compared with the same quarter a year earlier, and a growing market sets records by standing still. The streak’s end says less about the third quarter than about the base it was measured against. The 93 deals of the third quarter of 2025 were themselves a record. Falling 17% from a record is not the same as falling 17% from normal.

But the composition shift is harder to dismiss. When consolidators take 53% of deals, mega sellers set records, and small sellers fall to their lowest share ever, the market is not just pausing. It is changing shape. The firms that will sell best in 2027 are not the ones waiting for the count to recover. They are the ones using the pause to prepare: cleaning up the cap table, documenting the client relationships, and deciding, before a buyer asks, what the advisors actually want.

What the pause is for

Echelon Partners’ Sam Sphire put the preparation point plainly: sellers often do not know their options, what to ask potential partners, how to negotiate the challenging moments of a transaction, or how to position the firm in the areas that drive valuation. That ignorance is more expensive in a consolidator market than in a competitive one, because a consolidator buyer has seen a hundred firms and knows exactly which weaknesses to price. The seller who has not done the work pays for it in the multiple.

The third quarter’s lesson is therefore not that RIA M&A is cooling. It is that the market is sorting itself into two tiers, and the sorting is visible in the data: scale sells, small waits, and the buyer who can offer equity and continuity wins. For an owner considering a sale in 2027, the cheapest preparation window of the cycle is the quarter when everyone else is waiting. The deals announced in the fourth quarter will reflect decisions made in the spring. The decisions being made now, in the pause, are the ones that will set the record, or miss it, next year.
