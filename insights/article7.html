Earnouts in RIA Deals: What Sellers Actually Collect

A 12x offer with half the price in an earnout is not more money than a 9x all-cash offer. It is a different, riskier package — and the data on what it pays is sobering.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

The number was $24 million. That was the headline on the offer a founder of a $1.4 billion RIA received in the spring of 2026, twelve times his firm’s adjusted EBITDA, the kind of multiple that makes a career feel complete. The second offer on the table was $19 million. He took the $24 million. He did not read the second page closely enough: $9.6 million of his price was contingent, payable over three years if the firm kept its clients and grew revenue 12% a year. Two years in, the market has fallen twice, two senior advisers have left, and the earnout is tracking at less than half its maximum. The $19 million offer would have paid more.

His story is not unusual. It is the standard shape of an RIA sale in 2026. The headline price has never been higher — the median RIA deal was priced at 11.0x adjusted EBITDA in 2024, according to Sica Fletcher’s valuation data cited in Advisor Growth Strategies’ RIA DealRoom 2025 — and the share of that price actually paid at closing has never been lower. In the average 2024 deal, only 66% of the price was paid at closing. Ten percent arrived as retention payments. Twenty-four percent was contingent consideration, paid only if the business performed after the sale.

The question is not whether the headline is real. The question is how much of it a seller ever sees.

The short answer: about a fifth of the earnout dollar

There is no public dataset that tracks what RIA sellers specifically collect on earnouts — the terms are private, and the industry’s deal trackers record the headline, not the settlement. The closest hard data comes from SRS Acquiom, which administers post-closing payments on thousands of private-company deals and therefore sees the settlement side that deal-terms studies never capture. Its 2024 data, covering more than 850 private-company acquisitions worth $168 billion, is blunt: earnouts paid out about 21 cents on every dollar of maximum earnout written into the agreement. Fifty-nine percent of earnouts paid anything at all. Forty-one percent paid zero. At least 28% were contested, and of the deals that did pay, 17% needed a renegotiation to keep the parties out of court.

Those are all-industry numbers, and RIA deals differ from the average private-company sale in ways that cut both directions. The case for RIA earnouts paying better than average is retention: Schwab’s 2026 RIA Benchmarking Study puts annual client retention at 97%, and the asset a buyer is really purchasing — the client relationship — is stickier than a factory order book. The case for them paying worse is concentration: a handful of advisers and a handful of large clients determine whether the retention targets are met, and the data shows what happens when a firm changes hands. Firms that sold equity in 2023 lost clients at almost triple the rate of firms with no transaction that year — 8.4% attrition against 3.2%, according to the DealRoom analysis. The smaller the deal, the worse the payout record: for acquisitions of $50 million or less in upfront value, fewer than half of earnouts paid anything, and the median payment among those that did was 43 cents on the dollar, against 67 cents for larger deals.

Apply the 21-cent figure to a typical RIA structure and the arithmetic is stark. A $20 million headline with a 24% contingent slice — $4.8 million — is worth roughly $1 million in expected value. The seller who compares headlines is comparing fiction. The seller who compares expected cash is comparing deals.

How the structure got here

Earnouts were once the exception in RIA deals. They are now the mechanism by which the market’s record prices are financed. Cash at closing has compressed from a historical 80% to 90% of the purchase price to 40% to 70% in private-equity-backed deals, according to CT Acquisitions’ 2026 buyer guide, with the balance in rollover equity, seller notes, and earnouts of 20% to 40% tied to one-to-three-year retention. The driver is leverage: tougher lender underwriting and reduced loan-to-value thresholds have cut the amount of cash buyers can borrow, and the gap is filled with the seller’s own future performance.

Private equity now dominates the buyer side — Berkshire Global Advisors counted 225 RIA transactions in the first half of 2026, up nearly 40% year over year, with private-equity-backed acquirers accounting for about 85% of strategic acquisitions. A PE-backed buyer can pay the highest headline because it pays the least of it in cash. Allworth Financial, the Folsom, California acquirer that has bought more than 45 practices since 2018, typically pays selling owners 20% to 30% in Allworth equity and the rest in cash, WealthManagement.com reported in October 2024.

The earnout slice itself is getting bigger and shorter. SRS Acquiom’s 2026 study, drawn from more than 2,300 transactions worth $569 billion that closed between 2020 and 2025, puts the median earnout at 34% of the closing payment, up from 31% the year before, with a median performance period of about 24 months. Thirty-eight percent of earnouts run one to two years, 23% run a year or less.

What the earnout is actually measured on

For most RIAs, the earnout is pegged to revenue, because the firm’s EBITDA profile changes once it is absorbed into a larger entity that takes on costs. Revenue, in turn, is calculated on the market value of client assets. That chain — market to AUM to revenue to payout — is where the 2026 argument lives.

WealthManagement.com reported in March 2026 that sellers and their consultants are now pushing hard to strip market performance out of earnout growth models. “This wasn’t a conversation two years ago, but today it comes up in virtually every transaction,” said Kim Kovalski, a managing director at MarshBerry. “We’re pushing hard to negotiate these terms to better protect our clients.” Her test case: a team generating 20% organic growth should not be penalized because the market fell 30%. In some recent deals, buyers have agreed to exclude market performance from the growth models. Brooks Hamner, a senior vice president with Mercer Capital, told the same publication that the emphasis on normalizing growth metrics in seller contracts — especially in earnouts — is now a defining feature of the market.

The targets themselves are demanding. Richard Chen, writing on the Kitces platform, notes that earnout clauses focused on revenue growth commonly carry 10% to 15% compound annual growth targets. Over a three-year earnout, that is an obligation to grow revenue by 33% to 52%, with a quarter of the seller’s exit riding on it. A firm growing 5% organically that must hit 12% is not being asked to keep performing. It is being asked to transform, under new ownership, while its founder is negotiating his own exit.

There is also a structural detail that multiplies the ways to miss: 68% of earnout deals use multiple metrics, according to SRS Acquiom’s 2025 Deal Terms Study of more than 2,200 private-target acquisitions closed between 2019 and 2024. Revenue and EBITDA are the most common denominators — revenue in 47% of earnouts, EBITDA in 31% — with the remainder split across milestone and customer-retention measures. Only about 5% of deals require the buyer to run the business so as to maximize the earnout, per the American Bar Association’s Private Target Deal Points Study published in December 2025.

How sellers negotiate terms that actually pay

The negotiation has a clear playbook now, and it starts with the metric. Strip market beta out of the revenue target, so the seller is measured on net new assets and organic growth, not on the S&P 500. Replace cliffs with sliding scales: a seller who hits 80% of target should collect something — 50%, say — rather than zero. Add a mulligan year, a provision buyers have begun to accept, in which a short-term market downturn resets the measurement clock rather than killing the payout.

The second lever is the split between retention and growth. Retention payments — the 10% slice in the average 2024 deal, paid on AUM retained one to three years after closing — are the more collectible half of the contingent price, because they reward keeping what exists rather than building what does not. A seller who shifts the mix toward retention and away from growth-linked earnouts is trading upside for certainty, and in a market where the average earnout pays 21 cents, certainty is underpriced.

The third lever is control. The earnout is measured on numbers the buyer controls after closing: hiring, pricing, service levels, the integration timetable. A seller who negotiates operating covenants — minimum staffing, no repricing of the acquired book without consent, a defined integration plan — is not being difficult. He is protecting the only asset the earnout can be paid from.

And the fourth lever is the simplest: take less headline. A 10x offer with 80% cash at close beats a 12x offer with 40% cash and a 40% earnout on expected value, and it beats it by more once the time value and the dispute risk are priced in. The buyer who offers the higher headline knows this. The seller’s job is to know it too.

The case for the earnout, stated honestly

None of this makes earnouts a fraud. They are a legitimate bridge across a valuation gap: the seller believes the firm will keep growing, the buyer is not sure, and the earnout lets them both be right. Buyers say so themselves. The same WealthManagement.com piece quotes a buyer executive’s view that most buyers are long-term focused and “fully hope to pay the earn-out” — and for the best firms, the ones growing 20% organically with deep next-generation benches, the earnout is genuinely collectible.

The honest caveat runs the other way as well. The 21-cent figure is an all-industry average, not an RIA figure, and the RIA market’s 97% retention rate argues that the best RIA earnouts should beat it. But no one publishes RIA-specific payout rates, and the structure data we do have — a third of the average deal paid after closing, a quarter of it contingent on performance — points in the same direction as the SRS Acquiom data, not away from it. Until the industry publishes its own settlement numbers, the prudent seller treats the 21 cents as the base case and any better outcome as upside he has negotiated for, not upside he has been promised.

What to do with the headline

Three rules follow. First, value every offer on expected cash, not headline price: discount the earnout to a fifth of its maximum, discount the rollover equity for illiquidity, and compare what is left. Second, negotiate the metric before the price — a normalized, sliding-scale, retention-weighted earnout is worth more than a higher headline on a cliff-edge growth target. Third, remember who controls the denominator. An earnout measured on revenue the buyer manages, over a period the buyer sets, with no duty to maximize it, is not a price. It is a bet, placed with the seller’s money, on the buyer’s competence.

The founder with the $24 million offer learned this the expensive way. The next founder to sit at that table has the data. The headline is the advertisement. The cash at close, the retention terms, and the earnout metric are the deal.
