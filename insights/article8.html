Minority Stakes vs. Full Sales: What RIA Founders Give Up Either Way

A minority investor's check can be larger than a buyer's offer, and the founder keeps the chair. The price is control, paid in installments the founder may not notice until the second deal.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

The founder of a $2.1 billion RIA had two numbers on his desk in the spring of 2026. The first was a full-sale offer: $19 million, most of it in cash at closing, from a national consolidator. The second was a minority-stake investment: a check for roughly the same amount, at a valuation that implied his firm was worth more than the buyer had offered, from a private equity firm that promised to leave him in charge. He took the minority stake. Two years later, he wants to sell the rest of the firm, and he has discovered that the minority investor has a say in who buys it, at what price, and whether it happens at all. The check was real. The control he kept was conditional, and he did not read the conditions closely enough.

His story is the shape of the RIA deal market in 2026. Full sales are still the headline, but minority stakes have become the deal structure of choice for founders who want liquidity without giving up the chair, and for the private equity firms that have decided the best way to own an RIA is to own part of one and let the founder keep running it. The median RIA deal was priced at 11.6 times adjusted EBITDA in 2025, according to Advisor Growth Strategies’ RIA DealRoom 2025, up from 11.0 times in 2024, across sixty deals the firm tracked. DeVoe counted 322 RIA deals in 2025. Echelon counted 466, a number that includes minority stakes and the smaller transactions the other trackers miss. The market is not just growing. It is splitting into two markets, and the founder who cannot tell them apart will price his firm in the wrong one.

What a minority stake actually buys

A minority investment is, first, a check. The founder sells a portion of the firm, typically 20 to 40 percent, and takes cash out. The firm gets growth capital. The investor gets a seat at the table, usually a board seat, information rights, and consent rights over the decisions that matter most: selling the firm, raising more capital, hiring or firing the chief executive. The founder keeps the title, the office, and the day-to-day control. What the founder gives up is the right to decide the big questions alone.

The valuations are real. In June 2024, Advent International and the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority took a minority stake in Fisher Investments at a valuation of about $12.75 billion, roughly twenty times the firm’s trailing twelve-month earnings, while Ken Fisher retained more than 70 percent ownership and continued to run the firm. In September 2024, TPG invested roughly $2 billion in Creative Planning at a valuation above $15 billion. These are not distressed sales. They are the largest, most successful firms in the industry choosing to sell a piece of themselves at valuations that would have been unimaginable a decade ago.

The attraction is obvious. A founder who sells 30 percent at 11.6 times EBITDA takes home more cash than a founder who sells everything at 9 times, and keeps the job, the identity, and the upside. The minority investor brings capital for acquisitions, technology, and talent, and a network of portfolio companies that can become clients or referral sources. For a founder in his fifties who is not ready to retire, it looks like the best of both worlds: liquidity now, control retained, growth funded.

The math of the second bite

The case for the minority stake rests on a single idea: the second bite. The founder keeps 70 percent, the firm grows, and when the rest is sold in five or seven years, the remaining stake is worth far more than it is today. The arithmetic can work. It can also work against the founder, because the second bite is not a right. It is a negotiation, and the minority investor is sitting across the table in it.

The rollover equity that makes up the retained stake is the same instrument that dominates full sales, and the numbers are moving in the investor’s favor. Equity consideration averaged 29 percent of deal value in 2025, according to the same DealRoom data, and is expected to rise to 33 percent in 2026. In the typical transaction, roughly 70 percent of the price is cash at closing, with the remainder in earn-outs, equity consideration, and deferred retention payments. The founder who keeps equity is keeping the least liquid, least certain part of the deal, and the equity is held in the parent company or the aggregator, not in a public market. If the acquirer’s stock falls, the rollover loses value. If the acquirer goes public or is itself acquired, the rollover could be worth far more. The founder has no control over either outcome.

The minority stake adds a second layer to that risk. The investor’s consent rights mean the founder cannot sell the rest of the firm without the investor’s agreement, and the investor’s agreement comes at the investor’s price. The investor may prefer to hold, because the firm is growing and the investor’s return compounds. The investor may prefer a different buyer, because the investor’s fund has a relationship with them. The investor may simply want more of the firm at a valuation the founder would not accept from anyone else. The founder who took the minority check to avoid giving up control has given up the one control that matters most: the control over when and to whom the rest is sold.

What the full sale costs you

The full sale has its own price, and it is not small. The founder gives up the chair, the identity, and the upside. The firm’s name may survive, but the founder’s authority does not, and the non-compete that comes with the sale means the founder cannot simply start again. The earnout, which now runs 20 to 40 percent of the deal price, means a large share of the headline is contingent on performance after the sale, and across private-company sales generally, earnouts pay out about 21 cents on the dollar. A founder who sells everything at 11.6 times EBITDA may collect far less than the headline suggests, and the collection depends on people the founder no longer controls.

The full sale also has a virtue the minority stake does not: finality. The deal closes, the cash arrives, the founder walks away. There is no second negotiation, no partner with a veto, no five-year wait for the rest of the value. For a founder in his sixties who wants out, that finality is worth more than the upside the minority stake promises. The consolidator’s cash is certain. The minority investor’s second bite is a hope.

The case for selling everything — and the case against it

The counterargument to the minority stake is worth stating plainly, because it is the argument most founders actually make, and it is not wrong. A full sale is clean. The founder gets paid, the firm gets a new owner with scale and resources, and the clients get continuity under a larger platform. The minority stake, by contrast, leaves the founder running a firm in which someone else owns a large piece and has a say in its future, which is a partnership the founder did not choose and cannot easily end. For a founder who wants to retire, the full sale is the honest choice.

The counterargument fails for the founder who is not ready to retire, and that is most founders. A $2 billion firm built over twenty years is not a financial asset to its founder. It is a career, a reputation, and a team. Selling it all at once means giving up the thing that defines the founder’s working life, and the cash does not replace it. The minority stake lets the founder keep the work and take the money, and for a founder who loves the work, that trade is worth the conditions. The mistake is not taking the minority stake. The mistake is taking it without negotiating the conditions that will govern the second deal.

What to decide before you negotiate

The practical work starts before either offer arrives. A founder who is considering a minority stake should negotiate the exit before the investment: the right to force a sale of the whole firm after a fixed period, the right to buy out the investor at a defined valuation formula, and the consent rights the investor actually gets, written narrowly rather than broadly. The founder should understand that the rollover equity is the least liquid part of the deal and price it accordingly, and should insist that the minority investment be documented as a partnership with an end, not a marriage without one.

The founder who is considering a full sale should do the opposite work: negotiate the earnout down, the cash at closing up, and the non-compete narrowly, because the earnout is the part of the price most likely to disappear. Either way, the decision is not about the headline multiple. It is about what the founder gives up to get it, and whether the founder is giving up the chair, the upside, or the right to decide what happens next. A founder can give up any one of those. A founder who gives up all three has sold the firm twice and been paid once.
