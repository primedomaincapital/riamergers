How Long Does It Take to Sell an RIA, From First Call to Close?

Six months if everything is ready. Eighteen if it is not. And the clock that matters most starts years before any buyer calls.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

The call came on a Tuesday in March. A founder, sixty-one, running a $900 million practice he had built over twenty-six years, rang an M&A adviser he had met at a conference and asked a simple question: how long would this take? He wanted to be done by Christmas. He had a number in mind, a buyer already circling, and a daughter who had made it clear she did not want the firm. Nine months, he figured. Ten at the outside.

He closed the following June. Fifteen months after the first call, and only after a diligence fight over two institutional accounts, a renegotiated purchase agreement, and a client consent process that ran a full ninety days. He got his price — and a lesson: selling an advisory firm is not one transaction with a date on it. It is a sequence of gates, each with its own clock, and the gates do not run in parallel.

The honest answer to his question, the one advisers give when no buyer is listening, is six to twelve months from first buyer conversation to closing for a firm that is ready — and three to five years of preparation before that for a firm that wants the best price. The gap between those two numbers is where most sellers lose money, patience, or both.

The short answer, with the fine print attached

DeVoe & Company, the consultancy that has tracked RIA deal announcements for a decade, puts the lag in plain terms. Its founder, David DeVoe, told an audience at the firm’s M&A+ Succession Summit in September 2026 that the deals announced on any given day are the product of a decision to sell made six to eighteen months earlier. A professionally managed sale takes about six months from engagement to announcement. An owner working the process without an investment banker takes twelve to eighteen months. Private Wealth Daily, covering the same presentation, drew the blunt conclusion: the announced-deal count is a trailing indicator of seller sentiment, not a live one.

That six-to-eighteen-month spread is the first thing a seller should internalize. It is not a rounding error. It is the difference between hiring a banker and doing it alone, and it compounds: the owner who runs the process himself is out of the market for twice as long after any shock, which means the same market wobble costs him twice the calendar. The 2025 tariff shock and the 2026 conflict with Iran, DeVoe argued, are still working their way through the announcement data for exactly this reason.

Acquisition Stars, a practitioner guide for RIA owners, lands in the same band: an RIA sale commonly takes six to twelve months from first buyer conversations to closing. The firm’s September 2026 guide names the pacing item most sellers never budget for — the client consent process, which cannot begin until deal terms are settled and which typically runs forty-five to ninety days on its own.

Stage one: preparation, the three-to-five-year clock

The longest stage is the one that happens before any buyer is involved, and it is the stage sellers most often skip. David Grau Jr., chief executive of Succession Resource Group, told Longbridge in October 2026 that owners planning to retire and sell should start preparing three to five years in advance. His line was blunt: “Can you do this all in six months? Sure. Are you going to have to make compromises? Most certainly.” The work — internal succession plans, data organization, valuation, client portability — is what makes three to five years feel short. “Longer is better, shorter we can make work — but you just don’t get as much optionality.”

CT Acquisitions, an M&A advisory, frames it as a thirty-six-month playbook in its October 2026 guide. The first twelve months go to the value levers aggregators underwrite: AUM growth trajectory, fee-compression risk, the next-generation adviser bench. The middle twelve fix the pre-LOI diligence gaps. The final twelve turn a single inbound offer into a competitive process with real bargaining power.

This is the stage where the nine-month fantasy dies. A firm with a clean compliance history, an organized contract file, and a mapped consent process moves fast. A firm that starts that work after signing a letter of intent pays for it in calendar — and in leverage, because every week spent cleaning records is a week the buyer’s exclusivity clock is running.

Stage two: buyer search and the letter of intent

Preparation and buyer outreach together commonly take two to four months before a letter of intent is even signed, according to Acquisition Stars — particularly for a seller who has not previously organized the firm’s records for outside review. The LOI sets the preliminary price and terms, and it is the moment the process changes character: from a seller shopping a story to a buyer verifying one.

The market in late 2026 rewards sellers who arrive with options. DeVoe counted 77 RIA deals in the third quarter of 2026, down 17% from a year earlier, and now expects 320 to 340 deals for the full year against 2025’s record of 322. The buyer universe is not softening: more than forty aggregator buyers compete for high-quality sellers, and the firms that run a real process hold their price when the count dips.

Stage three: diligence and the definitive agreement

Grau puts the middle of the transaction — gathering the firm’s data, bringing it to market, negotiating, accepting an offer, doing due diligence, working on the contracts, lining up financing — at four to six months on its own. “You accept an offer, you do due diligence, we work on the contracts, we get the financing lined up,” he told Longbridge. “That can legitimately take four to six months.”

Diligence is where the buyer verifies what the LOI assumed, and it is where timelines quietly double. Unresolved compliance findings from a prior examination, a disorganized contract file, multi-state notice filings — each adds time independent of everything else, as Acquisition Stars notes. The quality-of-earnings review tests whether the cash flow behind the multiple is real; the compliance review tests whether the firm’s ADV, disclosures, and client agreements survive a new owner’s lawyers. A seller who prepared for three years sails through this stage in weeks. A seller who did not can spend months answering questions that should have been answered before the first buyer meeting.

Stage four: client consent, the ninety-day gate

Here is the stage with a hard legal floor. Section 205(a)(2) of the Investment Advisers Act requires every advisory contract to state that the adviser will not assign it without the client’s consent. In practice, most deals use negative consent: clients receive a notice describing the transaction and are deemed to consent if they do not object within a stated period, typically forty-five days or longer.

The notice cannot go out until the parties have largely finalized deal terms, since it has to describe the transaction clients are being asked to accept. Closing typically waits until the notice period has run, commonly forty-five to ninety days. That makes consent the pacing item on the back end of the deal — the long pole in the timeline, in Acquisition Stars’ phrase.

There is a trap inside the trap. If a meaningful share of clients require affirmative consent — institutional accounts, government clients, ERISA plans in particular — that portion of the timeline can extend well beyond the standard notice period. Affirmative consent means waiting for a signature, not waiting out a clock, and institutional sign-off runs on committee calendars.

Stage five: transition, the year after the wire clears

Closing is not the end of the timeline. Grau puts the transition process at six to twelve months, “maybe eighteen months” — the period when clients are introduced to the new firm, advisers are retained or lost, and the earnout clock, where one exists, starts running. The transition is where the deal’s economics are actually earned: retention payments and contingent consideration turn on whether clients stay, and clients stay when the handover is planned, not improvised.

Add it up and the full arc, from a standing start to a settled transition, runs eighteen months to two years for a prepared seller, and three to seven years counting preparation. The nine-month sale exists. It belongs to the founder who spent three years getting ready and then sold in nine months.

What stretches nine months into two years

Four delays account for most of the slippage. The first is the founder who starts preparing after the buyer calls: every diligence gap becomes a negotiation, and every negotiation happens under exclusivity. The second is the compliance surprise — an examination finding, a disclosure gap, a contract file that does not match the ADV — discovered by the buyer’s lawyers instead of the seller’s. The third is the consent mix: a book that looked retail turns out to hold institutional accounts that need affirmative sign-off. The fourth is financing. Tougher lender underwriting has cut the cash buyers can borrow, which means more of the price arrives as earnouts and seller notes, which means more to negotiate, which means more calendar.

The case for going fast anyway

None of this means a fast sale is a bad sale. Some founders have every reason to move quickly: a health event, a partner dispute, a buyer whose offer expires with the quarter. A six-month process with a banker, a clean file, and a retail client base that takes negative consent is a real thing, and the sellers who achieve it are not lucky — they are the ones whose preparation happened years earlier, invisibly, in the ordinary running of a well-kept firm.

The counterargument cuts the other way, too. A seller who rushes to market to catch a record multiple can find the market has moved while he was in diligence. DeVoe’s own data makes the point: the deals announced in a given quarter reflect decisions made six to eighteen months earlier. A seller who starts today is selling into the market of late 2027, not the market of today’s headlines. Speed bought by skipping preparation is not speed. It is a discount, collected later, in the form of a lower price, a longer earnout, or a transition that never quite settles.

What to do with the calendar

Three implications follow for any owner weighing a sale. First, start the preparation clock now, even if the sale is years away — the thirty-six-month playbook is a floor, not a stretch goal, and the firms that follow it sell faster when they finally do sell. Second, treat the consent process as a diligence item, not a closing formality: map every client into negative or affirmative consent before the LOI, and price the timeline accordingly. Third, hire the banker before the buyer calls. The six-month managed process and the eighteen-month solo process end at the same closing table. They do not end at the same price.

The founder who called in March got his deal done in fifteen months. He tells other founders it took nine. He is counting from the LOI. Everyone else in the room is counting from the first call — and the smartest sellers in the room started counting three years before that.
