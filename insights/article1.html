RIA M&A Valuations 2026: What Firms Are Actually Selling For

RIA valuations sit near decade highs, with median EBITDA multiples around 11x. What firms sell for by AUM tier — from 4x tuck-ins to 20x UHNW platforms.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

The first half of 2026 produced 167 announced RIA transactions — a pace that would make it one of the busiest years in the industry's history. And yet the most interesting number in wealth management M&A right now is not the deal count. It is the quiet question circulating among the acquirers writing the checks: how much longer can this last? Valuations sit near decade highs, buyers are paying prices their own investment committees call extraordinary, and a growing chorus — including major acquirers themselves — expects multiples to plateau or decline rather than climb further.

That tension is the market. Here is what firms are actually selling for, tier by tier, in the most expensive seller's market the RIA industry has ever seen.

The multiples, tier by tier

The headline benchmark: the median adjusted-EBITDA multiple on closed RIA deals reached 11.0x in 2024, up from 9.9x in 2023, according to Sica Fletcher's RIA Valuation Multiples Report — and stayed in the 11x–12x band through 2025 as competitive bidding by private-equity-backed aggregators kept pressure on platform prices. On a top-line basis, mid-2025 market commentary puts the typical revenue multiple in a 2.0x–4.0x band, with roughly 3.1x as the current industry average.

But the median conceals a market stratified by size, and size is the variable that matters most. The tiering, drawn from Mercer Capital and Sica Fletcher practitioner frameworks, looks like this:

Sub-$250M AUM — the lifestyle and tuck-in book — trades at 4–7x EBITDA or 1.0–1.8x revenue. These deals are typically priced on take-home cash flow plus the buyer's post-synergy margin rather than enterprise EBITDA. The buyer is acquiring a client list and a revenue stream to bolt onto an existing platform.

$250M–$1B AUM — the growth tuck-in — trades at 7–10x EBITDA or 1.8–2.7x revenue. At this scale the firm can plug into the buyer's compliance, HR, and technology stack without adding a back office, and the multiple reflects that operating leverage.

$1B–$5B AUM — platform-grade — trades at 10–14x EBITDA or 2.7–3.5x revenue, with $1B–$3B firms commanding 12–14x at the high end. This is the dominant deal-flow band: it represented 51% of all 2025 deals per DeVoe, and it is where more than 40 active aggregator buyers now compete for each high-quality seller, per Mercer Capital's 2025 analysis.

$5B+ AUM and UHNW/multi-family-office practices trade at 14–20x+ EBITDA, with Financial Advisor magazine reporting 20x EBITDA paid for top UHNW firms in 2024–25. These are scarcity assets — institutional-grade businesses with next-generation talent, and buyers pay accordingly.

The distribution tells its own story. Succession Resource Group's 2026 Advisor M&A Report found nearly 40% of deals transacted above 3.5x revenue, with more than 20% selling at 4.0x or greater. SRG's president David Grau Jr. offered the essential caveat: "The valuations paid today are higher than they've ever been before, but the same core fundamentals still apply — the deals have to cash flow." Record multiples, in other words, are being paid for large, well-run firms — and largely on terms.

What's actually changing hands: cash, paper, and promises

The multiple is the headline; the structure is the deal. Tougher lender underwriting and reduced loan-to-value thresholds have cut the amount of cash paid at closing — SRG attributes this directly to tighter credit. The gap is filled with contingent consideration: earnouts, seller notes, and rollover equity. Sellers can achieve a higher headline value than ever before, but most of that value is contingent on retention, growth, and performance over three to five years.

This is the detail that separates sophisticated sellers from disappointed ones. A 12x EBITDA offer with 40% in earnouts and a five-year retention handcuff is a fundamentally different transaction than a 10x offer with 80% cash at close. The industry's buyer-to-seller ratio — 61 buyers for every seller, per SRG, increasingly concentrated among fewer, larger, experienced acquirers — means sellers have leverage on price but buyers have leverage on structure. Casual and first-time buyers have largely exited; transactions are led by firms with established acquisition experience and access to capital, which makes for more competitive processes and more complex paper.

There is also a quiet repricing underneath the record volume. Private Wealth Daily's analysis of 2026 deal flow argues that record announcements partly reflect mandates signed before the repricing — the deal count describes the market that was, and the change shows up in how buyers pay for the next 500 deals: less cash up front, more retention-linked consideration, harder underwriting of the earnout triggers. The currency of RIA deals is shifting from AUM to cash flow and retention — buyers are paying for the parts of a wealth business that cannot resign.

The counter-signal: why smart sellers are pausing

Here is the contrarian read, and it comes from inside the buying community. Ted Jenkin of JPTD Partners, writing in Financial Advisor in September 2026 after surveying the first half's 167 transactions, framed the decision every founder faces: if you are 45, growing 20% organically, and love the work, holding may be the greatest investment you ever make. If you are 55, growing 5%, with 80% of your net worth in the business, and buyers are offering a historically extraordinary multiple — waiting a decade deserves a much harder look. His conclusion: RIAs will remain great businesses for a very long time; he just would not assume buyers will pay today's prices forever.

The math behind the caution is demographic and financial. Roughly a third of advisors are expected to retire within the next decade — the succession wave powering today's deal flow is finite. Interest rates rose again in September 2026, raising the cost of the borrowed money many acquirers lean on. And the buyer universe, for all its depth, is consolidating around platforms that are themselves being rolled up — sponsor-to-sponsor transactions where one private equity owner exits to a larger platform.

Today's multiple is not a valuation. It is a bid in an auction with forty paddles — and auctions end. For founders weighing a sale, the window is open, the bidding is fierce, and the terms have never mattered more.
