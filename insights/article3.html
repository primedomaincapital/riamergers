Selling Your RIA: Valuation Multiples Explained

What is your RIA worth? Revenue vs. EBITDA multiples explained, what moves your multiple up or down, and why deal structure matters more than headline price.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

Your firm does $4 million in revenue. One buyer offers $12 million. Another offers $20 million — with half of it in earnouts, a five-year employment agreement, and a non-compete that covers the county. Both are real offers. Only one of them is a real $20 million. Understanding the difference is the entire game of selling an RIA in 2026, and most founders learn it six months too late.

Valuation multiples look like simple arithmetic. They are actually a language — and like any language, fluency determines whether you get a fair price or a fluent-sounding bad deal. Here is the grammar.

Revenue multiples vs. EBITDA multiples — and which one matters

The two multiples measure different things, and serious buyers price on only one of them. A revenue multiple — enterprise value divided by annual revenue — is the industry's shorthand. It is quick, comparable across firms, and the number most often quoted in the press. In 2026, the typical RIA revenue multiple sits in a 2.0x–4.0x band, with roughly 3.1x as the current industry average; Succession Resource Group's 2026 data shows nearly 40% of deals transacting above 3.5x and more than 20% at 4.0x or greater.

But buyers underwrite on EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization — adjusted for the peculiar economics of founder-owned firms. The adjustments are where the real negotiation happens. Owner compensation gets normalized: if you pay yourself $300,000 but a hired CEO would cost $500,000, EBITDA is adjusted down. One-time expenses are added back. Above-market rent to a related entity, personal expenses run through the business, family members on payroll — all normalized. The resulting "adjusted EBITDA" is the earnings a buyer actually acquires, and the multiple applies to that number, not to the figure on your tax return.

This is why two firms with identical revenue can sell for wildly different prices. A $4 million-revenue firm running a 35% adjusted-EBITDA margin generates $1.4 million in earnings; at 10x, it is worth $14 million — a 3.5x revenue multiple. The same revenue at a 20% margin is $800,000 in earnings; at 10x, $8 million — a 2.0x revenue multiple. Margin is the multiple's silent partner. Buyers do not buy revenue. They buy earnings, and they pay for earnings they can grow.

For the smallest firms — sub-$250M AUM, trading at 4–7x EBITDA or 1.0–1.8x revenue — deals are often priced on take-home cash flow plus the buyer's post-synergy margin rather than formal enterprise EBITDA at all. The buyer is valuing what the book contributes after it lands on their platform, which is a polite way of saying the seller's standalone economics barely matter. Know which market you are in before you anchor on a multiple you read about a $3 billion firm.

What actually moves your multiple up or down

Within each AUM tier, five variables separate the top-quartile multiple from the median.

Growth rate. Nothing moves a multiple like organic growth. A firm compounding client assets at 15% organically will command a meaningfully higher multiple than a flat firm of identical size, because the buyer is purchasing a growth asset rather than a melting ice cube. Acquirers diligence this ruthlessly — they separate market appreciation from net new assets, because only the latter is repeatable.

Client demographics and concentration. An aging client base is a decaying revenue stream in slow motion; buyers model attrition. Concentration is worse: a book where the top ten relationships represent 40% of revenue carries key-person risk that gets priced as a discount — or deal-breaker. The premium goes to diversified, multi-generational books with documented next-generation relationships.

Recurring revenue quality. Fee-based advisory revenue on recurring billing is the gold standard. Transactional, commission-based, or project revenue is discounted heavily. The percentage of revenue that recurs without new sales activity is one of the first numbers a buyer calculates.

Next-generation talent. The industry's central anxiety is succession — a third of advisors retiring within a decade — and it cuts both ways. A firm with credentialed next-gen advisors under signed agreements, equity participation, and real client relationships commands a premium; it solves the buyer's retention risk. A firm that is one founder plus support staff is a key-person discount, whatever the AUM. SRG's data shows a 200% increase in firms deploying phantom or synthetic equity plans — the market's adaptation to this exact problem.

Enterprise quality. Clean financials, documented compliance history, institutional-grade technology, a real management team rather than a founder doing everything. The 61-to-1 buyer-to-seller ratio means buyers can afford to be selective, and enterprise quality is the filter. Firms that look like businesses sell like businesses; firms that look like practices sell like practices.

The structure behind the headline number

Here is the sentence to underline: a multiple is a price; the structure is the deal. Never confuse the two. In 2026's credit environment — tougher underwriting, reduced loan-to-value thresholds, a Fed rate hike in September — cash at closing has shrunk and contingent consideration has grown. The typical structure now blends cash at close, a seller note, rollover equity in the buyer's platform, and earnouts tied to retention and growth over three to five years.

Each element carries different risk. Cash is certain. Seller notes are subordinate debt — paid if the buyer's business performs. Rollover equity participates in the platform's upside, which has been lucrative for early sellers into platforms that subsequently recapitalized at higher valuations, and painful for those whose platforms stumbled. Earnouts are the most negotiated and most litigated: the triggers, the measurement methodology, who controls the client experience during the earnout period, and what happens if the buyer reorganizes your team in year two.

Tax treatment overlays everything — asset versus stock sale, allocation of purchase price, the character of earnout payments — and routinely moves the seller's after-tax proceeds more than a full turn of multiple. Get transaction counsel and a valuation specialist involved before the LOI, not after. The most expensive sentence in RIA M&A is "we'll figure out the structure later."

One final frame, borrowed from the buyers themselves. The question is not "should I sell?" It is: how much additional growth must you generate to justify the risk that today's multiple, today's tax rates, and today's buyer appetite will not be there when you are finally ready? In a market paying 11–12x EBITDA at the median and 20x for the best firms, that is a question worth answering with a spreadsheet rather than a feeling.
