The Great RIA Consolidation: Who's Buying and Why

Private equity backs three of four RIA deals in 2026. Who's buying wealth management — Focus Financial's hubs, Creative Planning's Europe push — and why.

Research · Prime Domain Capital

September 2026 was an instructive month in wealth management. Wealth Enhancement agreed to buy RWA Wealth Partners — a Boston firm with about $22.5 billion in client assets, the largest acquisition in Wealth Enhancement's history, letting Summit Partners exit its investment and creating a combined firm overseeing more than $187 billion. Carlyle took a majority stake in MAI Capital Management. Focus Financial kept stitching its network into regional hubs. Three deals, three different blueprints — and together, a portrait of an industry consolidating faster than at any point in its history.

The scale is difficult to overstate. Dakota tracked 238 announced transactions worth roughly $451 billion in client AUM across the first seven months of 2026 — 83 deals in Q4 2025, 102 in Q1 2026, 103 in Q2. Echelon's Q1 2026 report counted $1.67 trillion in transacted assets for the quarter alone, with 142 transactions announced. DeVoe counted 41 sales of firms managing more than $5 billion through September — already more than any previous full year, a record 17% of all deals. Echelon's median target firm grew 16.6% year over year to $733 million in AUM. This is not a wave. It is a sustained, multi-year distribution event.

The buyer map: three models of ownership

The first model is the mega-aggregator platform — the national brand buying tuck-ins and building density. Wealth Enhancement (backed by Onex and TA Associates) exemplifies it: the RWA deal is its largest ever, and the September transaction shows how capital now circulates within the ecosystem, with one sponsor's exit becoming another platform's growth. Beacon Pointe Advisors closed 12 deals in 2025 (DeVoe's #3 acquirer) and kept buying into 2026, including Litman Gregory Wealth Management ($2.7B) and The Financial Advisors ($1.2B). Carson Group crossed $50 billion in assets in 2025 after 21 acquisitions. Savant Wealth Management's acquisition of Heritage Financial Corp ($3.9B, 2026) was the largest in its history. EP Wealth set a record with 9 acquisitions in 2025.

The second model is the network consolidator — platforms rationalizing what they already own. Focus Financial Partners, under Clayton, Dubilier & Rice, spent 2026 accelerating intra-network combinations: Coastal Bridge Advisors' merger with Waddell & Associates and One Charles Private Wealth created a $10 billion RIA with a multi-market footprint, designed not just as a larger firm but as an acquisition vehicle for further affiliated practices. The strategy marks a philosophical shift: scale through operational integration — centralized technology, compliance, and business development — rather than simple asset aggregation. Fewer, larger, more durable hubs.

The third model is the strategic and international buyer. Creative Planning completed two international transactions in 2026: Swiss RIA Baseline Wealth Management ($1B+ AUM) in January and UK-based MASECO ($5B+ AUM) in March, as US acquirers push into Western Europe, Australia, and New Zealand — markets with similar structures, large pools of mobile wealth, and lower valuations than the US. Stone Point Capital invested in the UK's Amber River. Meanwhile WealthTECH M&A regained traction with 49 transactions in Q1 2026, up from 35 a year earlier, concentrated on AI workflow tools, client analytics, and compliance automation — capabilities firms are acquiring simply to keep pace.

Behind nearly all of it sits private capital. PE-involved activity reached 102 transactions in Q1 2026 — 71.8% of deals announced — driven by an all-time high of 95 PE-sponsored transactions, per Echelon. Fidelity's 2024 data put PE backing behind roughly 89% of closed RIA M&A, an all-time record. Sponsors increasingly deploy capital through platforms they already own rather than buying advisory firms directly; Dakota's Q2 2026 report found a disclosed PE or institutional sponsor behind roughly 70–75% of deal volume.

The engine underneath: succession, scale, and sponsor capital

Three forces power the machine, and none is slowing. First, succession. About a third of advisors are expected to retire within the next decade. AdvizorPro's August 2026 data shows the texture: of 28 acquired firms that month, 24 had four or fewer advisors and the median had just two; 20 managed under $500 million, with median AUM around $382 million. Consolidation is happening one boutique at a time — succession planning, capacity constraints, and the desire for a broader operating platform driving founders to sell. Modern Wealth's September acquisition of AWA Wealth Management ($290M) — its 24th transaction, six in nine months — was described as a succession deal priced in people: a founder with a date on the calendar and a transition plan.

Second, scale economics. A $500 million RIA cannot afford the technology stack, compliance infrastructure, and talent bench that clients now expect; a $50 billion platform amortizes them across thousands of advisors. Regulatory complexity, cybersecurity demands, and AI tooling costs all exhibit brutal economies of scale. The buyer is not just acquiring assets — it is acquiring the ability to operate at a cost structure the seller could never reach alone.

Third, sponsor capital needs deployment. Private equity raised enormous dry powder for wealth management, and the model requires continuous acquisition to generate returns. This creates the self-reinforcing cycle visible in 2026: sponsors fund platforms, platforms buy firms at 11–14x EBITDA, the acquired earnings justify the next fundraise, and eventually one sponsor exits to a larger platform — Summit Partners' RWA exit into Wealth Enhancement being the template. It works beautifully until the music of cheap leverage or willing sellers stops.

What the deal flow says about where this ends

Regulatory data sketches the endgame. Financial ADVSR's database, built from SEC Form ADV filings as of September 1, 2026, classifies 7,789 SEC-registered wealth managers as independent — not bank-owned, not public companies. Together they report $9.2 trillion in regulatory assets. The 237 firms with $5 billion or more — about 3% of the group — hold 56% of those assets. The 1,286 firms above $1 billion hold 79%. The median independent firm manages about $280 million.

Read that distribution forward ten years and the industry's shape is clear: a few dozen national platforms holding the great majority of assets, a long tail of boutiques that either sold, specialized deeply, or built genuine succession. The consolidation will not stop because the economics do not allow it to — but the terms will keep evolving toward retention-linked structures, and the bar for what counts as an attractive seller will keep rising. Consolidation is no longer about buying assets. It is about buying the last generation of founders' retirements — and the supply of those retirements, unlike sponsor capital, is finite.
